Read this before the headline

6 of 50 documents are empty

Six documents contributed zero predictions to this run. run_inference_with_retry exhausted its retries on a pass, asyncio.gather propagated the failure, and the whole document fell through to the empty-result branch — the report's own retry_failed_docs = 6. Their 110 intra gold positives (18.4% of the corpus total) are counted as false negatives against a model that was never allowed to answer.

The cause is not the model. _iter_json_arrays tracks string state so a ] inside a JSON string cannot close a block — but it counts every " in the response, including the ones in prose. When a pass writes — "Nothing bad is going to happen" means the rehab prevents… and the running quote count lands odd, the scanner believes the final answer array is inside a string, finds no arrays at all, and raises. 130 of 979 passes died this way. The baseline run had none: its terser, unexemplified reasoning simply used fewer quotation marks. The few-shot CoT style caused the bug to fire.

What the bug is worth

Re-aggregating the six documents from the passes that did survive (11 to 19 of 20 each) puts the run at 0.4669 instead of 0.4340. The bug costs 0.033 F1 — almost exactly the entire headline margin over the baseline. Every comparison below therefore uses the 44 documents both runs completed.

Pass by pass, against the baseline

44 documents · intra

Same 44 documents, same 3,302 candidate intra pairs, same 488 gold positives. The two runs land in very different places: few-shot trades precision for a lot of recall, and it is far less repeatable from pass to pass.

Mean single-pass F1 is 0.3877 for few-shot and 0.3840 for the baseline — a difference of 0.004, nothing. One pass of few-shot prompting is not a better classifier. What changed is the spread: false positives per pass swing 612→1070 (sd 116) against 460→692 (sd 57), and recall runs 0.57→0.71 against 0.45→0.58.

The finding: resampling stopped being pointless

+0.081 vs +0.024
  • 0.98The recall ceiling moved. Gold positives proposed by at least one pass: 97.9% for few-shot against 90.2% for the baseline. Only 10 gold links are never proposed by any pass, down from 48.
  • 0.41Misses stopped agreeing. Jaccard between two passes' false-negative sets falls from 0.565 (baseline) to 0.412. In the first audit the stable thing was what the model failed to see; that stability is what capped voting. Different examples now make different passes miss different links, which is precisely the raw material majority voting needs.
  • 0.79The vote count ranks better. AUC of the vote fraction rises from 0.764 to 0.793, and unanimous agreement is 43.7% precise against 36.8%.

Which example you show changes how much, not how well

z = +22 vs z = +1.2

_select_examples draws its examples per evaluation document, so "pass 7" is a different example on every document and pass index carries nothing across documents. The shared factor is the example document itself: each of the 40 pool documents appears on 16–29 evaluation documents, which is enough to estimate its own effect. Residualising each cell within its evaluation document and grouping by example gives a clean random-effects estimate, tested against a null that re-shuffles which example went with which pass.

And the part that matters

The same test on per-document F1 gives observed variance 0.0010 against a null of 0.0008 ± 0.0002 — z = +1.2, p = 0.14. There is no detectable good or bad example. The few-shot draw is a volume dial, not a quality dial, and the twenty passes are twenty roughly-equally-good voters set to twenty different volumes. That is the textbook condition for majority voting to help, and it is why the gain jumped from +0.024 to +0.081 while the mean single pass did not move at all.

Is there a better subset of examples?

no

A subset S is evaluated by restricting every document to the passes whose example is in S and voting with the framework's own rule — majority of whatever passes remain, ties to norel. Each of the 40 examples appears on 16–29 of the 44 documents, and each document draws 20 of them, so a subset of size m leaves m/2 passes per document. That confound has to be removed before any subset can be called good.

Where a smaller pool would be free

Decomposing a pass's output volume within its document: 27% of the variance is attributable to which example was shown, 73% is residual — the same example would still have produced a different answer. That share is an upper bound, since the 40 example effects were fitted on the same 880 cells. It means a pool of 8 examples spread over 20 passes would retain about 97% of the current pass-to-pass diversity, and a pool of 4 about 93%. If the CoT generation cost matters, cut the pool, not the passes. If F1 matters, add passes — the curve is still climbing at 20. What does not exist is a small set of specially good examples.

So which examples are the good ones?

no stable list

Greedy selection conflates an example's quality with the accident of which other examples it was picked alongside. A cleaner estimate: draw thousands of random subsets of a fixed size and, for each example, take the mean F1 of the subsets containing it minus the mean of those without. Subset size is held at 20, so the vote-count effect cancels and what remains is the example's own average marginal contribution.

The whole spread, best to worst, is +0.0082 to −0.0053 F1. No single example is worth anything on its own.

Selecting on this score does transfer, unlike greedy: estimated on half the documents and applied to the other half, the top 12 give 0.4508 against 0.4288 for a random 12 (t = +5.9) and 0.4182 for the bottom 12. That is worth roughly a doubling of the vote budget — top-12 at six votes per document beats a random 20 examples at ten. It is still below the 0.4647 you get by keeping all 40.

And nothing about the documents predicts it

Ten candidate characteristics were tested against the marginal contribution — none is significant, by Pearson, by Spearman, or by comparing the top ten against the bottom ten.

The raw top-10 means initially looked suggestive — 43.9 candidate pairs against 65.2, and a CoT answer showing 32 relations against 61 — but every one of those features is heavily right-skewed (skew +1.2 to +3.1), and the medians reverse or flatten thedifference entirely. Splitting the examples into quartiles by size gives mean ΔF1 of −0.0002, +0.0006, −0.0008, +0.0004: nothing. Whatever makes an example marginally useful is not its length, its event count, its causal density, the share of its gold that is intra-sentence, or how many relations its demonstration shows.

Which leaves a practical rule: you cannot pick good few-shot examples by inspection. If you want the +0.02 that selection is worth at small pool sizes, you have to measure marginal contributions on held-out documents and accept that the resulting list is half noise. If you want the full 0.465, keep all forty and spend the budget on passes instead.

Dependence between pairs: unchanged

still document-shaped

Everything the first audit found about pair dependence holds here, with the same magnitudes.

The mirror is still a duplicated variable — 2 asymmetric decisions in 11,198. Within-document clustering is if anything stronger (15.6× the Poisson-binomial variance, against 10.3×), correlation between decisions in the same sentence is unchanged at +0.33, and correlation across documents is still zero. Few-shot examples added a per-pass volume factor; they did not touch the spatial structure. Document-clustered bootstrap gives the 44-document F1 as 0.4655 ± 0.0225, and here clustering costs nothing relative to resampling decisions (design effect 0.97) because the extra variance is spread across documents rather than concentrated in a few.

Verdict

actionable
  • 1Fix _iter_json_arrays first. It is worth 0.033 F1 — the size of the whole few-shot effect as currently reported — and it silently deleted 12% of the corpus. Scanning from the last ] backwards, or preferring a fenced ```json block when one exists, removes the quote-parity dependence. Also worth decoupling the document from the pass: one failed pass should not discard the other nineteen (asyncio.gather(..., return_exceptions=True)).
  • 2The few-shot gain is real at +0.061 on matched documents, 95% CI [+0.019, +0.102], P(Δ>0) = 0.997 under a document bootstrap. Report it that way, not as 0.434 vs 0.401.
  • 3Keep 20 passes here. The opposite of the baseline recommendation: the curve is still rising at m=20, so more passes would still pay. Spend on passes while the examples stay distinct.
  • 4Drop the threshold tuning. Majority is already optimal for this run; the k≥6 recommendation from the first audit was specific to the baseline's correlated voters.
  • 5Do not go looking for the best example. The per-example F1 effect is indistinguishable from noise (z = +1.2). Diversity of examples is doing the work, so selection effort is better spent guaranteeing spread than ranking candidates.
  • 6One caveat on per-decision accuracy. McNemar over individual decisions is 180 vs 220 against few-shot (p = 0.051): it is right about fewer pairs overall while scoring much better F1, because it says far more. If a downstream consumer cares about precision rather than recall, this run is not an improvement.
